10 Things to Consider Before Buying Land in Florida (That Most Buyers Miss)

Most land-buying checklists cover the basics: confirm road access, get a survey, and make sure the title is clean. Those still matter, and our Complete Guide to Buying Land in Florida walks through them step by step.

But Florida land comes with its own quirks. The state's geology, wildlife, water rules, and tax system all affect what you can do with a piece of property and what it will cost you to own it. If you're looking at land for sale in Florida, here are 10 things worth looking into before you make an offer.

1. Know How You'll Use the Land Before You Shop

The right parcel for a weekend hunting camp is not the right parcel for a full-time homestead. Before you start comparing listings, decide what the land is for:

  • A future home needs buildable ground, a practical route for power, and soil that will support a septic system.
  • Horses or livestock need open pasture (or a budget to clear it), fencing, and a reliable water source.
  • Recreation and hunting favor wooded acreage, wildlife cover, and privacy.
  • A long-term investment depends most on location, access, and how fast the surrounding area is growing.

Knowing your use case narrows the search quickly and keeps you from paying for features you don't need.

2. Zoning and the Future Land Use Map

Every Florida county has a zoning code, but it also has a comprehensive plan with a Future Land Use Map that sets the long-term vision for each area. Zoning tells you what is allowed today. The future land use designation tells you what the county intends for the area, and it can limit how many homes you can build per acre or whether a parcel can be split later.

Call the county planning and zoning office with the parcel ID and ask three questions: What is it zoned? What is the future land use designation? Can I build a home, keep animals, or place a manufactured home here? Also ask the seller whether the property carries any deed restrictions, which can add rules on top of the county's.

3. Wetlands and Flood Zones

Florida has more wetlands than most states, and a parcel can look dry in October and hold standing water in July. Two things to check:

  • FEMA flood zone. Look the parcel up on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. A parcel's flood zone can affect how and where you build, and what insurance costs.
  • Wetlands. Building in or near wetlands can require extra permits. Ask the county what applies before you plan a homesite.

A parcel with some wetland acreage isn't a dealbreaker. It's often great for wildlife. Just make sure your planned homesite sits on high, dry ground.

4. Soil, Septic, and the "Perc" Question

Most rural Florida land isn't served by city sewer, so your home will likely need a septic system. Whether you can install a conventional system depends on the soil and the depth to the water table. Poor soil or a high water table can require a mounded or engineered system, which can cost significantly more.

Before you buy land you plan to build on, ask whether a soil evaluation has been done. If not, you can hire a licensed septic contractor or engineer to do one, and your county can tell you what the septic permitting process looks like.

5. Water: Wells and Water Management Districts

Without city water, you'll need a private well. Ask the county what permits are needed to drill one on the property.

Well costs vary with depth and local geology, so ask a local well driller what nearby wells typically run. Neighbors with existing wells are a great source of information, too.

6. Sinkholes and Karst Terrain

Much of Florida sits on limestone, and that's what gives the state its springs, caves, and crystal-clear rivers. It's also why sinkholes happen. Areas with karst geology, including much of North Florida and the Panhandle, can have depressions or older sinkholes on the property.

Walk the land and look for circular depressions, sudden dips, or ponds with no obvious inlet. If you're planning to build, a geotechnical evaluation of the homesite is inexpensive peace of mind compared to the cost of the home itself.

7. Gopher Tortoises and Protected Wildlife

This one surprises a lot of out-of-state buyers. Gopher tortoises are a protected species in Florida, and they love the same dry, sandy, high ground people like to build on. If you find tortoises or their burrows where you plan to clear or build, check with the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC) about what's required before work begins.

Look for burrows, which are half-moon-shaped openings in sandy soil, when you walk a property, and allow extra time in your plans if you see them.

8. Property Taxes, Homestead, and the Agricultural Classification

Florida's tax rules can work in your favor if you understand them:

  • The homestead exemption only applies to your permanent residence. Vacant land doesn't qualify until you've built on it and live there full time.
  • Agricultural classification (often called "greenbelt") can significantly lower property taxes on land used for bona fide agricultural purposes, such as cattle, hay, or timber. It's not automatic. You apply through the county property appraiser, and the use has to be genuine and ongoing.

Look up the parcel's current tax bill on the county property appraiser's website, and ask whether it currently carries an agricultural classification. If it does and you don't continue that use, expect the taxes to go up.

9. Easements, Mineral Rights, and Timber Rights

The deed tells you what you own. The title commitment tells you what other people may have rights to. Read it carefully for:

  • Utility and access easements that let others cross or use part of your land
  • Reserved mineral rights held by someone other than the seller
  • Timber rights or leases that may let a previous owner or company harvest trees after you buy

None of these are automatically bad, but you want to know about them before closing, not after. A title company or real estate attorney can explain anything you don't understand.

10. The Real Cost of Getting the Land Ready

The purchase price is only the beginning. If you plan to build or move onto the land, budget for:

  • Power. Ask the local electric co-op or utility how far the nearest line is. Extending service a long distance can be expensive.
  • Clearing and a driveway. Pine and hardwood clearing, culverts, and gravel add up quickly on a long drive.
  • Well and septic, as covered above.
  • Building to Florida code. Florida's building code is designed with hurricanes in mind, which can affect construction cost and design.
  • Insurance. Get a homeowner's insurance quote early, as coverage in Florida varies a lot by location and construction type.

Getting rough estimates on these items before you buy helps you compare parcels honestly. A cheaper parcel that needs a half mile of power line may cost more in the end than one with power at the road.

Bonus: Visit the Land More Than Once

Photos and drone video are great for narrowing your list, but nothing replaces walking the property. Visit after a heavy rain to see how it drains, drive the route at different times of day, and talk to the neighbors. They'll tell you things no listing ever will.

The good news is that most of these questions are easy to answer if you ask them early. Work with a seller who specializes in land, and they'll often have the answers ready before you even ask.
